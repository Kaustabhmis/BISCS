Profit Margin vs Markup: How to Calculate Both (With Examples)

Calculate profit margin and markup from cost and selling price, set a price for a target margin and avoid the classic margin-versus-markup pricing mistake.

By BISCS India Editorial Team Updated 2 November 2026 5 min read
Quick answer

Profit margin is profit as a percentage of the selling price, while markup is profit as a percentage of cost. An item bought for ₹750 and sold for ₹1,000 has a 25% margin but a 33.33% markup. Profit margin and markup both describe profit, but against different bases. Margin is profit as a share of the selling price: (price − cost) ÷ price × 100. Markup is profit as a share of the cost: (price − cost) ÷ cost × 100.

Buy a product at ₹750 and sell it at ₹1,000, and you make ₹250 profit. That is a 25% margin but a 33.33% markup. Mixing these up is one of the most common pricing errors in small businesses. The free Profit Margin Calculator shows both numbers side by side.

Margin and markup formulas

Measure Formula ₹750 cost, ₹1,000 price
Profit Price − Cost ₹250
Margin Profit ÷ Price × 100 25%
Markup Profit ÷ Cost × 100 33.33%
Price from margin Cost ÷ (1 − margin) 750 ÷ 0.75 = ₹1,000
Price from markup Cost × (1 + markup) 750 × 1.3333 = ₹1,000

Because margin uses the bigger number (price) as its base, it is always smaller than markup for the same profit.

The classic pricing mistake

Ritu runs a small home-décor shop in Jaipur. A lamp costs her ₹700. She wants a 30% margin, so she adds 30% to the cost and prices it at ₹910.

But ₹210 profit on a ₹910 price is a margin of only about 23.08%. To get a true 30% margin she needs to price at 700 ÷ 0.70 = ₹1,000. On 200 lamps a year, the difference is ₹18,000 of profit she thought she was earning.

Quick margin-to-markup conversion

Target margin Markup needed
10% 11.11%
20% 25%
25% 33.33%
33.33% 50%
50% 100%

The formula is markup = margin ÷ (1 − margin), using decimals. For example, a 40% margin needs 0.40 ÷ 0.60 = 66.67% markup.

Example: the real margin on a marketplace sale

Margins shrink quickly when you sell online. Here is a hypothetical product sold through a marketplace, with all figures excluding GST:

Item Amount
Selling price ₹1,000
Product cost ₹600
Marketplace commission (15%) ₹150
Shipping ₹60
Payment collection fee (2%) ₹20
Profit ₹170

Looking only at product cost, the margin seems to be 40%. Once commission, shipping and fees are added to the cost (₹830 in total), the margin is 17%. To get this in the calculator, enter ₹830 as the cost price and ₹1,000 as the selling price. Returns, packaging and advertising would reduce it further, so build them in when you set prices. Commission and fee rates differ by platform and category, so use the figures from your own seller agreement.

Margins for service businesses

Freelancers and agencies can use the same idea with time as the cost. If a designer's monthly costs, including their own target salary, come to ₹90,000 and they bill about 120 hours, each hour costs ₹750. Pricing at ₹1,000 an hour gives a 25% margin. Choosing Price from margin with ₹750 as cost shows the hourly rate needed for any target margin.

When to use margin and when to use markup

  • Use markup when you set prices from cost, for example a trader adding a fixed percentage to purchase price.
  • Use margin when you look at profitability, compare products or talk to lenders and investors, since financial statements report margins on sales.
  • Use both when negotiating with distributors and retailers, who may quote "margin" but mean markup. Always confirm which one is meant.

What happens when costs rise?

If your supplier raises the cost from ₹750 to ₹800 and you keep the price at ₹1,000, your margin drops from 25% to 20%. To restore a 25% margin, the new price is 800 ÷ 0.75 = ₹1,066.67. Checking this every time a purchase price changes keeps small cost increases from silently eating your profit.

Pricing tips for small businesses

  1. Price before GST. Work out margin on the taxable value, then add GST with the GST Calculator.
  2. Include all variable costs. Packaging, shipping, marketplace commission and payment gateway fees all reduce profit per unit.
  3. Plan for discounts. If you run a 20% sale, a 25% margin almost vanishes. Test sale prices with the Discount Calculator and recheck the margin here.
  4. Know your break-even. At a selling price equal to cost, margin is 0%. Anything less is a loss.
  5. Review regularly. When supplier prices rise, recalculate before your margin quietly erodes.

Common mistakes

  • Treating markup as margin, as in Ritu's example.
  • Calculating margin on GST-inclusive prices, which overstates revenue.
  • Forgetting returns, damage and unsold stock when estimating real profit.
  • Expecting a 100% margin to be possible; it is not, because profit cannot exceed the price.
  • Using percentage figures without checking them against actual rupees. The Percentage Calculator helps with quick checks.

Limits of the calculator

The tool works per unit using the cost and price you provide. It does not allocate overheads, account for taxes on profit or model volume discounts. Its margin is therefore closest to a gross margin unless you build overheads into the cost.

This guide is general information to help with pricing arithmetic, not accounting or tax advice. For statutory accounts, GST treatment of discounts and schemes, or business valuation, consult a chartered accountant, and confirm GST rules on the GST portal or CBIC website.

Frequently asked questions

How do I find the selling price for a target margin?

Divide the cost by (1 minus the margin as a decimal). For a 30% margin on a cost of ₹700, the price is 700 ÷ 0.70 = ₹1,000. Adding 30% to cost gives only ₹910, which is a margin of about 23%. The calculator's Price from margin mode does this for you.

Can profit margin be more than 100%?

No, not for a positive price. Margin is profit divided by price, and profit can never exceed the price, so the margin is always below 100%. Markup, on the other hand, can be any size: buying at ₹100 and selling at ₹300 is a 200% markup but a 66.67% margin.

Should I include GST when calculating margin?

For a GST-registered business, calculate margin on prices excluding GST, because the GST you collect is passed on to the government after adjusting input tax credit. If you are not registered and cannot claim input credit, the GST you pay on purchases is part of your cost.

Is this gross margin or net margin?

The calculator gives a margin on whatever cost you enter. If you enter only the purchase or production cost, the result is close to gross margin. To estimate net margin, include a share of overheads such as rent, salaries, delivery, payment fees and marketing in the cost per unit.
