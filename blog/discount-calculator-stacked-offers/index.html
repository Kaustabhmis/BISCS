Discount Calculator: Final Price After Stacked Discounts

Work out the sale price and your savings after one or two discounts, understand why 20% plus 10% is not 30% off, and see where GST fits into the final price.

By BISCS India Editorial Team Updated 2 November 2026 5 min read
Quick answer

To find a sale price, multiply the original price by (1 − discount ÷ 100). Two discounts apply one after the other, so 20% plus an extra 10% on ₹2,999 gives ₹2,159.28, an effective 28% off rather than 30%. To find the price after a discount, multiply the original price by (1 − discount ÷ 100). A ₹1,499 shirt at 40% off costs 1,499 × 0.60 = ₹899.40. Your saving is the original price minus the final price.

When a shop offers an "extra" discount on top of a sale price, the second discount applies to the reduced price, not the original. So 20% off followed by an extra 10% off is 28% off in total, not 30%. The free Discount Calculator on BISCS India handles one or two discounts and can add GST for prices quoted before tax.

Worked example: a festive sale

A pair of running shoes has an MRP of ₹2,999. The store offers 20% off, and your bank card gives an extra 10% off.

  1. After 20% off: 2,999 × 0.80 = ₹2,399.20
  2. After the extra 10%: 2,399.20 × 0.90 = ₹2,159.28
  3. Total saving: 2,999 − 2,159.28 = ₹839.72
  4. Effective discount: 839.72 ÷ 2,999 × 100 = 28%

A straight 30% discount would have cost ₹2,099.30, about ₹60 less. That gap grows with bigger discounts.

The stacked discount formula

For two discounts a% and b%, the effective discount is:

Effective % = 1 − (1 − a ÷ 100) × (1 − b ÷ 100), then × 100

20% + 10% 30% 28%
30% + 20% 50% 44%
50% + 20% 70% 60%
50% + 50% 100% 75%

On a ₹4,000 item, "50% + extra 20% off" means paying ₹1,600, while a true 70% off would mean ₹1,200. Reading offers this way helps you compare deals across stores and apps.

How do you find the original price from a sale price?

Sometimes you only see the sale price and the percentage. Divide the sale price by (1 − discount ÷ 100). A dress tagged ₹1,799 "after 40% off" should have an original price of 1,799 ÷ 0.60 = ₹2,998.33, so roughly ₹2,999. If the tag claims an MRP far above that, the advertised discount does not add up.

Are "buy one get one" offers really 50% off?

Only if you wanted two of the item. Buy one get one free on two items priced the same is a 50% discount per item. "Buy 2 get 1 free" is one free item in three, or 33.33% off. "Buy 3 get 1 free" is 25% off. If you only needed one, a straight 20% discount may save you more money than a bigger-sounding multi-buy deal. To compare, work out the price per item you actually need and run the straight-discount option through the calculator.

Where does GST come in?

For goods sold in shops and online to consumers, the MRP printed on the pack is inclusive of all taxes, and the sale price you pay is also tax-inclusive. You do not add GST on top.

The GST option is useful in other situations: a supplier quoting "₹50,000 less 10%, plus GST", or a service priced before tax. In those cases, GST is generally charged on the discounted value shown on the invoice. With 18% GST, the shoes in our example would come to ₹2,159.28 + ₹388.67 = ₹2,547.95. Smart shopping checklist

  • Is the discount on the MRP or on an already-inflated "original" price? Compare with the price a few weeks earlier if you can.
  • Are the two offers stacked or combined? Most "extra" discounts are stacked.
  • Is a bank or wallet discount capped at a maximum amount? A "10% up to ₹500" offer gives only ₹500 off a ₹10,000 purchase, which is 5%.
  • Do delivery charges, convenience fees or cash-on-delivery fees reduce the saving?
  • Is the item returnable at the sale price, and are exchange terms the same?

Tips for shop owners running a sale

Discounts cut directly into profit. If your margin on a product is 25%, a 20% discount leaves you with very little. Before announcing a sale, check the new margin in the Profit Margin Calculator. Common mistakes

  1. Adding discounts together. 20% + 10% is 28% off, not 30%.
  2. Ignoring caps on card offers. The headline percentage may apply only up to a small rupee limit.
  3. Adding GST to an MRP-based price. MRP already includes tax.
  4. Comparing deals by percentage alone. Compare the final rupee price, including fees.
  5. Rounding the first step. Keep paise until the end for an accurate final price.

A note on accuracy

The calculator applies discounts in the order entered and GST on the final discounted amount. Actual offers can include caps, minimum order values, or conditions that change the result. This guide is general information for everyday arithmetic, not legal or tax advice. If you think a retailer has charged above MRP or misrepresented an offer, the National Consumer Helpline and the Legal Metrology department in your state are the right places to raise it.

Frequently asked questions

Is 20% plus 10% off the same as 30% off?

No. The second discount applies to the already-reduced price. On ₹1,000, 20% off leaves ₹800, and a further 10% off leaves ₹720, which is 28% off in total. A straight 30% discount would leave ₹700. The effective discount is 1 − (0.8 × 0.9) = 28%.

How do I calculate the discount percentage from two prices?

Subtract the sale price from the original price, divide by the original price and multiply by 100. If a jacket marked ₹2,500 is selling for ₹1,750, the discount is 750 ÷ 2,500 × 100 = 30%. This helps you check whether a sale tag's claim is accurate.

Is GST added on top of the discounted price?

In shops, MRP already includes all taxes, so the discounted price you pay normally includes GST too. GST is added separately mainly on business quotes and some services priced before tax. Where it is added, it is generally charged on the price after any discount shown on the invoice.

Does the order of two discounts matter?

No. Because each discount multiplies the price, 20% then 10% gives the same final price as 10% then 20%. What matters is whether the discounts are stacked, as most extra offers are, or added together into one bigger percentage, which is rarer. Check the offer's terms.
