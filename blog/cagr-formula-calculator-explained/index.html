CAGR Calculator: Compound Annual Growth Rate Made Simple

Find the compound annual growth rate between two values, project a future value from a CAGR and learn why CAGR differs from average and absolute returns.

CAGR, or compound annual growth rate, is the constant yearly rate at which a value would have to grow to get from its starting point to its ending point over a given number of years. The formula is CAGR = (final value ÷ initial value)^(1 ÷ years) − 1.

If your mutual fund investment of ₹1,00,000 is worth ₹2,50,000 after 5 years, the CAGR is (2.5)^(1/5) − 1, about 20.11% a year. The free CAGR Calculator on BISCS India does this sum and can also work the other way, projecting a future value from a growth rate.

Why use CAGR instead of total return?

Total (absolute) return tells you how much something grew in all, but not how fast. A 150% gain sounds impressive, but it means something very different over 5 years than over 15. CAGR puts every investment or business metric on the same yearly scale so you can compare them fairly.

It also smooths out the bumps. Real investments rarely grow at the same rate every year. CAGR answers a simpler question: "What steady yearly rate would have produced this result?"

Worked examples

What is measured Start End Years CAGR
Mutual fund lump sum ₹1,00,000 ₹2,50,000 5 20.11%
Shop's annual revenue ₹40 lakh ₹65 lakh 4 12.91%
Flat bought and sold ₹45 lakh ₹72 lakh 9 5.36%
A share price ₹500 ₹800 3 16.96%

The flat example is a useful reality check. A ₹27 lakh gain feels large, but spread over nine years it is about 5.36% a year, before stamp duty, registration, maintenance, brokerage and capital gains tax. Comparing that rate with what a deposit or fund earned over the same period puts the result in perspective.

Projecting forward works too: ₹1 lakh growing at a CAGR of 15% for 7 years reaches about ₹2,66,002.

CAGR vs average return: why it matters

Imagine an investment of ₹1 lakh that gains 50% in year one (₹1.5 lakh) and loses 50% in year two (₹75,000). The simple average of +50% and −50% is 0%, which suggests you broke even. In fact you lost ₹25,000. The CAGR is about −13.4% a year, which matches your real experience.

This is why fund factsheets and company reports quote CAGR rather than averages for multi-year performance.

When CAGR is the right tool, and when it is not

Good uses:

  • Comparing lump-sum investments held for different periods.
  • Measuring growth in sales, profit, users or prices between two dates.
  • Checking the real annual growth of a property, gold or a share you bought and sold.
  • Setting a realistic return assumption for the Lumpsum Calculator.

Poor uses:

  • SIPs or any investment with several deposits or withdrawals; use XIRR instead. The SIP Calculator projects SIPs from an assumed return.
  • Judging risk. Two investments can have the same CAGR but very different ups and downs along the way.
  • Very short periods, where a single good or bad month can distort the yearly rate.

Common mistakes

  1. Dividing total return by the number of years. 150% ÷ 5 = 30% is not the CAGR; the correct figure is about 20.11%.
  2. Getting the years wrong. Count the actual time held. Three years and six months is 3.5, not 3 or 4.
  3. Ignoring costs and taxes. CAGR on prices excludes brokerage, stamp duty, fees and tax unless you include them in the values.
  4. Ignoring inflation. A 6% CAGR when prices rose 6% a year is roughly no real growth. Check with the Inflation Calculator.
  5. Assuming past CAGR will continue. It describes history, not the future.

Limits and a reminder

CAGR is a single summary number. It hides volatility, the timing of gains and the order of returns, and it says nothing about the risks you took. The calculator handles values of zero or more for the final amount and any positive duration, but negative starting values are outside what the formula can describe.

This article is general information for understanding growth rates, not investment advice. Before acting on past performance figures, check official fund documents on AMFI or your fund house's site, and consider guidance from a SEBI-registered adviser.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between CAGR and absolute return?

Absolute return is the total percentage gain over the whole period, ignoring time. CAGR spreads that gain into an equivalent yearly rate. Growing from ₹1 lakh to ₹2.5 lakh is a 150% absolute return, but over five years that is a CAGR of about 20.11% a year.

Is CAGR the same as average annual return?

No. The simple average of yearly returns can be misleading. If an investment rises 50% one year and falls 50% the next, the average is 0%, but you have actually lost 25% of your money. The CAGR over those two years is about −13.4%, which reflects what really happened.

Can I use CAGR for SIP investments?

Not accurately. CAGR assumes a single starting amount and a single ending amount. A SIP has many investments on different dates, so its return is usually measured with XIRR, which accounts for each cash flow. Use CAGR for lump sums, prices, NAVs, revenue or other start-and-end values.

Can I enter a period that is not a whole number of years?

Yes. Duration can be fractional, such as 5.5 years for five years and six months. A shorter or longer period changes the result noticeably: ₹1 lakh growing to ₹2.5 lakh in 5.5 years is a CAGR of about 18.13%, compared with 20.11% over exactly 5 years.
