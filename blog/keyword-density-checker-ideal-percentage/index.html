Keyword Density Checker: What Is a Healthy Percentage?

Check how often words and phrases appear in your content, spot keyword stuffing and find missing topics. Includes what density really means for SEO today.

By BISCS India Editorial Team Updated 2 November 2026 4 min read
Quick answer

Keyword density is the number of times a word or phrase appears divided by the total word count, shown as a percentage. Keyword density tells you how often a word or phrase appears compared with the total number of words on a page. If "home loan" appears 12 times in a 1,000-word article, its density is 1.2%.

The Keyword Density Checker on BISCS India counts every word and phrase in your text, sorts them by frequency and checks a focus keyword against a sensible range. The useful part is not the number itself but what it reveals: repetition you did not notice, or a topic you meant to cover and barely mentioned.

Does keyword density still matter for SEO?

Not in the way it did fifteen years ago. Modern search engines understand synonyms, related concepts and context. A page about "two-wheeler insurance" can rank for "bike insurance" without repeating that exact phrase twenty times.

What still matters:

  • The topic must be clear. If your main subject barely appears, neither readers nor search engines will be sure what the page is about.
  • Over-repetition hurts. Google's spam policies name keyword stuffing explicitly, and repetitive text simply reads badly.
  • Related terms help. A guide to GST returns that never mentions GSTR-1, GSTR-3B or input tax credit is probably thin.

So use density as a diagnostic, not a target. The checker treats roughly 0.5–2.5% as a healthy range for a focus keyword, flags values above about 4% as stuffing risk, and tells you when the keyword is missing entirely.

Reading the results sensibly

The top of the single-word list usually shows your main topics. If it is dominated by words like "best", "service" or your company name, the copy may be more about selling than explaining.

The two- and three-word views are often more revealing. They show the phrases you actually lean on. You might find "our company provides" twelve times, which is a sign to tighten the writing, or discover that "income tax slab" appears only once in an article meant to rank for it.

Phrases never cross sentence or comma boundaries, so the counts reflect real phrases rather than accidental word pairs.

What the numbers mean

Focus keyword density What it usually means What to do
0% The phrase is not used at all Mention it naturally in the opening and a heading
Below about 0.5% Light usage Fine for long articles; add a mention if it fits
About 0.5–2.5% Natural usage Leave it alone and focus on quality
About 2.5–4% Getting repetitive Swap some instances for synonyms or pronouns
Above about 4% Likely keyword stuffing Rewrite the affected paragraphs

These ranges are practical guidelines, not rules from Google. A 150-word product description will naturally have a higher density than a 2,000-word guide.

Common mistakes when optimising for keywords

  • Writing for a percentage. Adding the keyword to every sentence to "reach 2%" makes content worse.
  • Ignoring variations. People search for "EMI calculator", "loan EMI calculator" and "how to calculate EMI". Covering these naturally is better than repeating one form.
  • Stuffing location names. "Best plumber Kolkata, plumber in Kolkata, Kolkata plumber services" in every paragraph is a classic pattern Google's spam policies describe.
  • Counting the whole page source without stripping it. Navigation menus, footers and cookie notices can distort the counts. Paste only the main content when you want a precise view.
  • Forgetting the title and headings. Density only measures body text. The title tag, H1 and subheadings carry more weight for showing what a page is about.

Tips for natural, well-covered content

  • Mention the main topic early, ideally in the first paragraph.
  • Use the exact phrase in one or two headings where it reads naturally.
  • Answer the questions people actually ask about the topic. The Question & Topic Generator can suggest them.
  • Read the text aloud. If a phrase sounds repetitive, it is.
  • Check readability as well as frequency. The Readability Checker highlights long sentences and passive voice that make content harder to follow.

Frequently asked questions

What is the ideal keyword density for SEO?

There is no official ideal figure, and Google does not rank pages by hitting a percentage. As a rough sense check, a main keyword used naturally often lands somewhere between about 0.5% and 2.5%. Much higher than that usually reads as repetitive and can look like keyword stuffing.

Does Google penalise keyword stuffing?

Yes. Google’s spam policies specifically list keyword stuffing, meaning filling a page with keywords or numbers in an attempt to manipulate rankings. Pages doing this can rank lower or be removed. Writing naturally, with synonyms and related terms, avoids the problem.

Can I check a live web page with this tool?

The tool does not fetch websites. Open the page in your browser, view the page source, copy all the HTML and paste it in. The checker strips tags, scripts and styles automatically and analyses only the visible text.

Does the tool work for Hindi or other Indian languages?

Yes. Words are split using your browser’s language-aware segmenter, so Devanagari, Bengali and other scripts are counted as words. There is also an option to ignore common Hindi and Hinglish stopwords such as hai, ka and ki.

Why are words like the and and missing from the list?

By default the checker ignores common English stopwords because they dominate every text and tell you nothing about its topic. Untick the stopword option if you want to see the full raw frequency list.
