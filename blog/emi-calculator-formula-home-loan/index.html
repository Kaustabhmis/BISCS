EMI Calculator: Formula, Examples and Amortisation Explained

Work out the monthly EMI on a home, car or personal loan, see how much interest you pay over the tenure and read a year-by-year amortisation schedule.

By BISCS India Editorial Team Updated 2 November 2026 4 min read
Quick answer

EMI is calculated as P × r × (1 + r)^n ÷ ((1 + r)^n − 1), where P is the loan, r the monthly interest rate and n the number of months. A ₹25 lakh home loan at 8.5% for 20 years costs about ₹21,696 a month. BISCS India's free EMI Calculator also shows total interest and a yearly amortisation table.

An EMI (equated monthly instalment) is the fixed amount you pay every month to repay a loan with interest. It is calculated with the formula EMI = P × r × (1 + r)^n ÷ ((1 + r)^n − 1), where P is the loan amount, r is the annual rate divided by 12 and by 100, and n is the tenure in months.

For example, a ₹25 lakh home loan at 8.5% a year for 20 years works out to about ₹21,696 a month. Over 240 months you repay roughly ₹52.07 lakh, so about ₹27.07 lakh of that is interest. The free EMI Calculator shows these numbers instantly and breaks them down year by year.

How is EMI calculated, step by step?

Take the same ₹25 lakh loan:

  1. Monthly rate: 8.5 ÷ 12 ÷ 100 = 0.0070833
  2. Months: 20 × 12 = 240
  3. Growth factor: (1.0070833)^240 ≈ 5.44
  4. EMI: 25,00,000 × 0.0070833 × 5.44 ÷ (5.44 − 1) ≈ ₹21,696

You do not need to do this by hand, but seeing the steps helps you understand why the rate and tenure matter so much. The factor (1 + r)^n grows quickly with both, which is why small changes have a big effect over 20 years.

How do rate and tenure change your EMI?

Here is the same ₹25 lakh loan under three scenarios, calculated with the tool:

Scenario Monthly EMI Total interest Total paid
8.5% for 20 years ₹21,696 ₹27.07 lakh ₹52.07 lakh
8.5% for 15 years ₹24,618 ₹19.31 lakh ₹44.31 lakh
9.0% for 20 years ₹22,493 ₹28.98 lakh ₹53.98 lakh

Two lessons stand out. Cutting five years off the tenure raises the EMI by about ₹2,900 but saves nearly ₹7.8 lakh of interest. And a half-percent higher rate adds about ₹800 to each EMI, which becomes almost ₹1.9 lakh over 20 years.

For a shorter loan, such as a ₹5 lakh personal loan at 10.5% for 5 years, the EMI is about ₹10,747 and total interest about ₹1.45 lakh.

Reading the amortisation schedule

In the early years, most of each EMI pays interest. In month one of the home loan above, interest is about ₹17,708 and only about ₹3,987 reduces the principal. The balance falls slowly at first and then faster, because each month a little less interest is due.

This is why prepayments early in the loan have the biggest effect. Every rupee of principal you clear in year two stops attracting interest for the remaining 18 years. The schedule's "Loan paid" column shows how far along you are; on the ₹25 lakh example, only about 12% of the principal has been repaid after the first five years.

When should you use an EMI calculator?

  • Before applying: to check what loan size your monthly budget can carry. Many lenders look at how much of your income goes to EMIs, so a lower EMI can affect eligibility.
  • When comparing offers: enter each lender's rate and tenure to compare total interest, not just the EMI.
  • After a rate change: enter the outstanding balance, new rate and remaining months to see the new EMI.
  • When planning a prepayment: reduce the loan amount by your prepayment and compare the outcomes of keeping the EMI or the tenure the same.

Mistakes people make with loan EMIs

  1. Looking only at the EMI. A lower EMI from a longer tenure usually means far more interest.
  2. Ignoring fees. Processing fees, insurance bundled with the loan and GST on fees add to the real cost but are not part of the EMI formula.
  3. Assuming a floating rate stays fixed. Model a rate rise of 1% or so to see if you could still manage.
  4. Forgetting the tax angle. For a self-occupied home, interest on a home loan may be deductible up to ₹2 lakh a year under the old tax regime. Try the numbers in the Income Tax Calculator before assuming a benefit.
  5. Comparing flat-rate and reducing-balance quotes directly. Some lenders quote a flat rate, which charges interest on the original amount throughout. The Simple Interest tool shows how flat interest is worked out, and it is usually much costlier than the same reducing-balance rate.

What the calculator does not cover

The tool assumes a fixed rate for the whole term and equal monthly instalments starting one month after disbursement. It does not model step-up EMIs, moratoriums, part-prepayments in the middle of the schedule or broken-period interest. For a full picture of how money grows or costs compound, the Compound Interest calculator is a useful companion.

Treat these results as estimates for planning, not as a loan offer or financial advice. Your sanction letter and the lender's repayment schedule are the official figures, and RBI rules and your loan agreement govern rate resets and charges. If a decision is significant, discuss it with your bank or a qualified adviser.

Frequently asked questions

Does a longer tenure reduce the total cost of a loan?

No. A longer tenure lowers each EMI but increases total interest, because you borrow the money for longer. On ₹25 lakh at 8.5%, a 15-year term costs about ₹19.3 lakh in interest while a 20-year term costs about ₹27.1 lakh, even though the EMI drops by roughly ₹2,900.

Why is most of my early EMI going towards interest?

Interest each month is charged on the outstanding balance, which is highest at the start. As the balance falls, the interest part shrinks and the principal part grows. In the first month of a ₹25 lakh, 8.5%, 20-year loan, about ₹17,708 of the ₹21,696 EMI is interest.

Will my bank's EMI match this calculator exactly?

It should be very close for a fixed-rate loan, but small differences are normal. Banks may round the EMI, charge broken-period interest from the disbursement date, or use a slightly different day count. Processing fees, insurance and floating-rate resets are not included in a standard EMI formula.

What happens to my EMI if the interest rate rises on a floating loan?

Lenders usually either increase the EMI or keep it the same and extend the tenure. Extending the tenure can add many months and a lot of interest. You can model a rate change by entering the outstanding balance, the new rate and the remaining months in the calculator.

Is a zero-interest EMI really free?

Not always. Some no-cost EMI offers build the interest into the product price or pass on a discount you would otherwise have received, and processing fees or GST on interest may apply. Compare the total amount you will pay with the price for an outright purchase before deciding.
