Inflation Calculator: Future Cost and Value of Money in India

See what today's expenses will cost in future and what a future sum is worth in today's rupees, with the inflation formula, Indian examples and planning tips.

By BISCS India Editorial Team Updated 2 November 2026 5 min read
Quick answer

Inflation raises prices at a compound rate, so future cost = today's cost × (1 + inflation)^years. At 6% a year, something costing ₹1 lakh today will cost about ₹1.79 lakh in 10 years, and ₹1 lakh received then is worth about ₹55,800 in today's money. Inflation is the rise in prices over time, which means the same rupee buys less each year. To see what something will cost in future, use future cost = today's cost × (1 + inflation rate)^years. To see what a future amount is worth today, divide instead: today's value = future amount ÷ (1 + inflation rate)^years.

At 6% inflation, a ₹1,00,000 expense today will cost about ₹1,79,085 in 10 years. Looked at the other way, ₹1,00,000 received in 10 years will buy only what about ₹55,839 buys now. The free Inflation Calculator on BISCS India shows both answers and a year-by-year table.

How inflation compounds

Inflation works like compound interest in reverse for your savings. If prices rise 6% this year, next year's 6% is applied to the already-higher price. Over a decade, ₹1 lakh of spending grows like this at 6%:

  • After 1 year: ₹1,06,000
  • After 5 years: about ₹1,33,823
  • After 10 years: about ₹1,79,085

That is a 79% rise in ten years, even though 6% a year sounds mild. Put another way, ₹1 lakh loses about 44.2% of its purchasing power over that decade.

Planning examples

Goal Today's cost Inflation assumed Years Future cost
General household expense ₹1,00,000 6% 10 ₹1,79,085
Child's annual college fee ₹2,00,000 8% 15 ₹6,34,434
Monthly living costs at retirement ₹50,000 6% 25 ₹2,14,594

The last row often surprises people. A couple in Bhubaneswar who live comfortably on ₹50,000 a month today may need over ₹2.1 lakh a month for the same lifestyle in 25 years, at 6% inflation. Retirement planning that ignores this can fall badly short.

How much is ₹1 lakh worth in future?

This table shows what ₹1,00,000 received in the future would buy in today's prices, at three inflation rates:

Inflation rate Worth today if received in 10 years Worth today if received in 20 years
4% ₹67,556 ₹45,639
6% ₹55,839 ₹31,180
8% ₹46,319 ₹21,455

Two things stand out. First, the difference between 4% and 8% inflation is enormous over 20 years: the same rupee amount is worth more than twice as much in one case as in the other. Second, long-dated promises such as a fixed sum from an insurance policy maturing in 20 years are worth far less than the headline suggests. Before buying any product that pays a fixed amount decades away, divide that amount by the inflation factor here to see its real value.

Why inflation matters for your money

  • Savings in low-interest accounts lose value in real terms if the interest is below inflation.
  • Fixed deposits can barely keep pace once tax is deducted. Check your FD's maturity with the FD Calculator, then convert it to today's money here.
  • Long-term goals like education, a house or retirement need targets set in future rupees, not today's.
  • Salary hikes below inflation mean a real pay cut, even if the number on your payslip rises.

Is your salary hike beating inflation?

A quick real-pay check: divide (1 + hike) by (1 + inflation) and subtract 1. If Anjali's salary rises 5% in a year when prices rise 6%, her real pay change is 1.05 ÷ 1.06 − 1, about −0.94%. Her payslip shows more rupees, but they buy slightly less than last year's salary did. Over several years of below-inflation increments, the gap adds up, so it is worth running your own numbers before an appraisal conversation.

Common mistakes

  1. Using today's prices for a goal 15 years away. The target will be far too low.
  2. Applying one inflation rate to everything. Education and healthcare costs have often risen faster than general prices.
  3. Adding inflation instead of compounding it. 6% for 10 years is not 60%; it is about 79%.
  4. Confusing nominal and real returns. A 10% return with 6% inflation is a real return of roughly 3.8%, not 4%, because (1.10 ÷ 1.06) − 1 ≈ 3.77%.
  5. Ignoring tax on returns. Tax reduces what is left to fight inflation.

Choosing a sensible rate

Over long periods, India's CPI inflation has often been in the region of 5–6% a year, but it has swung well above and below that in individual years. The RBI's inflation target is 4%, with a tolerance band of 2% either side. For personal planning, many people use 6% as a base case and test 7% or 8% for a cautious view, with higher figures for school and college fees.

Limits of the calculator

The tool applies one steady rate to every year. Real inflation varies year to year and differs by city, household and category. The result is an illustration of scale for planning, not a forecast. This article is general information rather than financial advice; for official data, refer to the CPI releases from the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation and the RBI, and consider professional advice for major financial plans.

Frequently asked questions

What inflation rate should I use for planning?

There is no single right rate. Many people use around 6% for general household costs as a planning assumption, and higher rates for education and healthcare, which have often risen faster than average. Try two or three rates to see a range rather than relying on one number.

What is the difference between future cost and today's value?

Future cost tells you how much money you will need later to buy what costs a given amount now. Today's value works the other way, telling you what a future sum can buy in current prices. The calculator has a mode for each, using the same compound formula.

How does inflation affect my fixed deposit returns?

Your real return is roughly the interest rate minus inflation, and after tax it is lower still. If an FD pays 7% and inflation is 6%, your purchasing power grows by only about 1% a year before tax. For someone paying tax on that interest, the real return can be negative.

Where can I find official inflation figures for India?

The Consumer Price Index (CPI) is published monthly by the National Statistics Office under the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation. The Reserve Bank of India uses CPI inflation for its monetary policy target. Both websites publish current and historical data you can use to choose a rate.
