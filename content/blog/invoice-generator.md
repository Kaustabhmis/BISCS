A GST tax invoice is the bill a registered business issues when it supplies goods or services. It must show the **supplier's and buyer's details and GSTINs**, a **unique invoice number and date**, the **place of supply**, **HSN or SAC codes**, the **taxable value**, and the **tax charged**. That tax is split as **CGST + SGST** when the supplier and the place of supply are in the same state, and charged as **IGST** when they are in different states.

**Note:** this guide explains general GST invoicing practice as at the time of writing. GST rules and rates change, so confirm specifics for your business on the official GST portal or with a chartered accountant.

## CGST + SGST or IGST: the core rule

The deciding question is whether the supply is **intra-state** or **inter-state**:

| Supplier's state | Place of supply | Supply type | Tax charged |
|---|---|---|---|
| West Bengal | West Bengal | Intra-state | CGST + SGST, each half the rate |
| West Bengal | Karnataka | Inter-state | IGST at the full rate |
| Delhi | Delhi | Intra-state | CGST + SGST (Delhi has its own legislature) |
| Maharashtra | Chandigarh | Inter-state | IGST |

In union territories without their own legislature, such as Chandigarh, an intra-state supply attracts **CGST + UTGST** instead of SGST.

The **place of supply** is decided by GST law. For goods it is generally where the movement of goods ends, and for many services to registered businesses it is the recipient's location. Unusual cases, such as events, transport or exports, have their own rules, so check if you are unsure.

The total tax is the same either way. Only the heads change, which affects where the tax is credited and how the buyer claims input tax credit.

## What must a GST tax invoice contain?

Under the GST invoice rules, a tax invoice should include:

- Supplier's name, address and GSTIN
- A consecutive **invoice number**, unique for the financial year, of up to 16 characters
- **Date** of issue
- Buyer's name and address, and **GSTIN if registered**
- **Place of supply** with the state name, for inter-state supplies
- **HSN** (goods) or **SAC** (services) code
- Description, **quantity** and unit
- **Taxable value** after any discount
- **Tax rate** and amount for CGST, SGST/UTGST or IGST
- Whether tax is payable on **reverse charge**
- **Signature** of the supplier or authorised signatory

## E-invoicing: when a PDF is not enough

Businesses whose aggregate turnover in any financial year exceeds the notified threshold must issue **e-invoices** for B2B supplies. At the time of writing that threshold is **₹5 crore**. E-invoicing means reporting each invoice to the government's Invoice Registration Portal, which returns an **IRN** and a signed QR code to print on the invoice.

This tool does not connect to the portal or generate IRNs. If you are above the threshold, use e-invoicing through the official portal or your accounting software, and check the latest threshold and timelines on the official e-invoice website.

## Common GST invoicing mistakes

- **Charging IGST on a local sale,** or CGST + SGST on an out-of-state one.
- **Wrong place of supply,** especially when the billing and delivery addresses are in different states.
- **Reusing or skipping invoice numbers** within a financial year.
- **Missing the buyer's GSTIN** on B2B invoices, which can block their input tax credit.
- **Applying GST before the discount** instead of on the discounted taxable value.
- **Using a tax invoice when composition or unregistered,** which is not allowed.
- **Wrong HSN/SAC or rate,** which causes mismatches in returns.
