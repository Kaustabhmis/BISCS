Body Mass Index (BMI) is your weight in kilograms divided by the square of your height in metres. A person who is 170 cm tall and weighs 68 kg has a BMI of 68 ÷ (1.70 × 1.70) = 23.5.

Whether 23.5 counts as "healthy" depends on which scale you use. Under the general WHO categories it is in the healthy range, but under the lower cut-offs commonly used for Asian Indian adults it falls just into "overweight". The free [BMI Calculator](/tools/bmi-calculator/) shows you either scale with one tick box.

**Important:** BMI is a quick screening estimate, not a diagnosis. It does not measure body fat directly, and it should not replace advice from a doctor or registered dietitian.

## What is the BMI formula?

The formula is the same everywhere:

**BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height (m)²**

If you measure in feet and pounds, convert first, or let the calculator do it. For example, 5 feet 7 inches is 67 inches, or 1.70 m, and 150 lb is about 68.0 kg. That gives a BMI of about 23.5, the same as the metric example above.

## BMI categories: WHO and Asian Indian cut-offs

Research has found that people of South Asian origin tend to have more body fat, especially around the waist, and a higher risk of type 2 diabetes and heart disease at a lower BMI than many other populations. For this reason, guidelines commonly cited in India use lower thresholds for adults.

| Category | WHO (general) | Asian / Indian cut-offs (commonly cited) |
|---|---|---|
| Underweight | Below 18.5 | Below 18.5 |
| Healthy weight | 18.5 – 24.9 | 18.5 – 22.9 |
| Overweight | 25 – 29.9 | 23 – 24.9 |
| Obese | 30 and above | 25 and above |

Different sources word these slightly differently. Some Indian guidance, for example, puts the lower end of normal at 18.0 rather than 18.5. The broad message is the same: for Indian adults, a BMI from 23 upwards deserves attention earlier than the WHO scale would suggest. Your doctor may also use other measures before drawing conclusions.

## Worked example: the same person on two scales

Take Rahul, 170 cm and 68 kg, BMI 23.5.

- **WHO scale:** healthy. His healthy weight range is about 53.5 kg to 72.0 kg.
- **Asian cut-offs:** overweight, just above the 22.9 limit. His healthy range becomes about 53.5 kg to 66.2 kg, so the tool suggests losing about 1.8 kg to reach it.

Now take Priya, 160 cm and 82 kg. Her BMI is 82 ÷ 2.56 = 32.0, which is obese on both scales. The calculator shows a target that brings her into the healthy range, but a safe plan and pace should come from a doctor or dietitian, not from a number alone.

## What BMI cannot tell you

BMI is popular because it needs only a tape and a scale. Its limits are just as important:

- **It ignores body composition.** Muscle weighs more than fat for the same volume, so sportspeople and gym regulars can read as "overweight" while being fit.
- **It does not show where fat sits.** Fat around the abdomen carries more health risk than fat elsewhere.
- **It is for adults only.** Children and teenagers need age- and sex-specific growth charts.
- **It is not meant for pregnancy.** Weight gain during pregnancy follows different guidance.
- **It can miss risk in older adults,** who may lose muscle while keeping the same weight.

### Add a waist measurement

Many clinicians pair BMI with waist circumference. Thresholds commonly cited for Indian adults are around 90 cm for men and 80 cm for women, above which abdominal obesity is considered more likely. Measure midway between your lowest rib and the top of your hip bone, breathing out normally.

## Common mistakes when checking BMI

- Using height in centimetres without converting to metres (68 ÷ 170² gives a meaningless 0.0024).
- Weighing after a heavy meal or in shoes and outdoor clothes.
- Relying on an old height measurement. Adults can lose a little height with age.
- Reading a single result as a diagnosis rather than a reason to check further.
- Comparing your number with a friend's who uses a different scale.

## What to do with your result

If your BMI is in the healthy range, keep up regular activity and a balanced diet, and recheck every few months. If it is below or above the range:

- **Talk to a doctor** for a basic check-up. Blood pressure, blood sugar and cholesterol tests give far more insight than BMI alone.
- **See a registered dietitian** for a plan that fits your food habits, budget and any medical conditions.
- **Aim for gradual change.** Sudden crash diets are hard to sustain and can be unsafe.
