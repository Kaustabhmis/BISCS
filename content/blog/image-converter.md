## JPG, PNG or WebP: which format should you use?

Each format was designed for a different job. Choosing the right one matters more than any quality slider.

| Format | Best for | Transparency | Typical file size | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| JPG (JPEG) | Photos, form uploads, printing | No | Small | Text and sharp edges can blur slightly |
| PNG | Screenshots, logos, graphics, cut-outs | Yes | Large for photos | Big files when used for camera photos |
| WebP | Websites and apps | Yes | Smallest | A few older portals and programs still reject it |

A simple rule: photos go to JPG (or WebP for websites), anything with flat colours, text or a transparent background goes to PNG.

## Common conversions and when they make sense

### PNG to JPG

This is the most common one. Phone screenshots and some scanner apps save as PNG, which can be several megabytes for a single image. Many exam and job portals in India only accept JPG for photo and signature uploads. Converting fixes both issues in one step.

If the PNG has a transparent background, such as a signature cut-out, the transparent area must become a solid colour in JPG. Choose white for forms.

### JPG to PNG

Converting a JPG to PNG does **not** improve its quality. The compression artefacts already in the JPG stay there, and the file usually grows. It makes sense only when the next program in your workflow requires PNG, or you plan to edit the image repeatedly and do not want further JPG losses each time you save.

### Anything to WebP

If you run a website, an online shop or a blog, WebP is usually the most efficient choice for both photos and graphics, and it supports transparency. Page speed matters for visitors on mobile data, so converting your product images to WebP is a worthwhile habit.

### WebP to JPG

Images saved from websites often turn out to be WebP. When an older desktop program, a print shop or a government portal refuses the file, converting it to JPG solves the problem straight away.

## What happens to quality when you convert?

JPG and WebP are **lossy**: they discard detail the eye is unlikely to notice. The quality slider controls how much is thrown away. At very low settings you will see blocky patches in skies and smudged edges around text.

PNG is **lossless**: it stores every pixel exactly. That is why the quality slider does not apply to PNG output, and why PNG photos are large.

Converting from a lossy format to a lossless one cannot bring back lost detail. Converting the other way, PNG to JPG, loses a little each time. For important images, keep the original file and convert from that rather than converting a converted copy.

## Tips for clean conversions

- **Use the right tool for size targets.** If a form says the photo must be under a certain number of KB, use [Compress Image](/tools/image-compressor/), which searches for the quality that fits. Converting alone may not land on the number.
- **Check dimensions too.** Converting does not change width and height. If the portal also specifies pixels, use [Resize Image](/tools/image-resizer/) first.
- **Keep logos in PNG.** Converting a logo with crisp edges to JPG tends to add faint halos. PNG or WebP keep it clean.
- **Batch your work.** If you are preparing thirty product photos for a website, add them all at once and download a ZIP rather than converting one at a time.
- **Animated GIFs become still images.** The converter works on a single frame, so an animation will not survive conversion to JPG or PNG.

## Common mistakes

- Converting a JPG to PNG expecting it to look sharper. It will not, and the file will be bigger.
- Saving a transparent logo as JPG and getting a black or white box around it.
- Uploading WebP to a portal that only lists JPG and PNG, then wondering why the upload button greys out.
- Setting quality to 100% for every photo. The file grows a lot for almost no visible gain over 90%.
- Renaming a file from `.png` to `.jpg` by hand. That changes the label, not the content, and some portals will detect the mismatch.
