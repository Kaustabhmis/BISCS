A strong YouTube title tells a viewer, in a glance, what the video is about and why it is worth their time. Put the main keyword near the start, keep the important part within about 60–70 characters, and make a promise the video actually keeps.

That sounds simple until you are staring at an empty title box after a long edit. The [YouTube Title Generator](/tools/youtube-title-generator/) gives you a page of ideas from your topic in a second, so you can pick, tweak and check one instead of starting from nothing.

## What makes a YouTube title work

Viewers decide whether to click in a fraction of a second, usually while also looking at the thumbnail. Titles that do well tend to share a few traits:

- **The topic comes first.** "Excel Pivot Tables for Beginners" is clearer than "Here's Something Every Office Worker Should Learn About Excel Pivot Tables".
- **There is a clear benefit or angle.** A how-to, a list of mistakes, an honest review or a comparison each sets an expectation.
- **It matches the video.** If the title promises ten tips, deliver ten tips. Viewers who feel tricked leave early, and YouTube's policies discourage misleading metadata.
- **It reads naturally.** Write for a person scrolling on a phone, not for a keyword list.

The thumbnail and title work as a pair. Avoid repeating the exact thumbnail text in the title; use the title to add the detail the image cannot.

## Reading the length badges

YouTube accepts titles of up to 100 characters, but far fewer are visible in most places. The generator labels each idea so you can see this at once:

| near the search limit | 61–70 | Usually fine, but check the ending |
| may be cut off in search | 71–100 | Keep key words at the front |

## Turning an idea into your final title

The generated titles come from templates, so treat them as drafts. A good routine:

- Star five or six that fit your video, then read them aloud. Drop any that sound like a sales pitch.
- Replace generic words with specifics from your video: a model name, a price, a place, a result.
- Check that the first four or five words still make sense on their own, since that is all some viewers will see.
- Make sure the title and thumbnail together answer "what is this and why should I watch?"

If you have two strong options, note both. You can change a title later in YouTube Studio, and some channels have access to YouTube's built-in title and thumbnail testing.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Clickbait that the video does not support.** It may win a click but tends to lose the viewer quickly, which hurts more than it helps.
- **Stuffing several keywords in a row.** "Excel Tutorial Excel Tips Excel for Beginners" reads like spam.
- **Burying the topic after a long hook.** On narrow screens the hook is all anyone sees.
- **Adding a year to evergreen content.** A 2024 label on a timeless tutorial makes it look stale in 2026.
- **Copying a viral title word for word.** Borrow the structure, not the wording, and make it true to your own video.
- **Ignoring Shorts conventions.** Shorts titles are often seen briefly while swiping, so short and direct usually works best there.

## Tips for Indian creators

Many Indian viewers search in English, Hindi or a mix, sometimes in the same session. If your video is in Hindi or Hinglish, try a Roman Hindi title alongside an English one and see which feels more natural for your audience. Adding "in India" makes sense when prices, rules or availability are India-specific, such as a phone launch or a tax change, but it adds little to a general cooking or fitness video.

Regional creators can also use the audience and topic fields to mention a city or state, for example "Kolkata Street Food Tour" or "Darjeeling Weekend Trip on a Budget". Specific titles often face less competition than broad ones.
