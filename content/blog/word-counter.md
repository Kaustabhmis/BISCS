A word counter tells you exactly how long your text is: the number of words, characters, sentences and paragraphs, and roughly how long it takes to read or say out loud. Paste your text into the box and the numbers update as you type.

That sounds simple, but the count is often the thing standing between you and a rejected application, a cut-off caption or an essay that loses marks for being too long. Here is how to use one well, and what the different numbers actually mean.

## When does an exact count matter?

Plenty of everyday tasks in India come with a hard limit. A few common ones:

- **College and scholarship essays**: statements of purpose often specify a range such as 500 to 800 words. Going over can look like you ignored instructions.
- **Board exam and university answers**: practising to a target length helps you plan time in the exam hall.
- **Job application forms**: "Describe yourself in 150 words" boxes on careers portals sometimes cut off text silently.
- **Social media**: X (formerly Twitter) has a 280-character limit for standard posts at the time of writing, and Instagram captions are capped at 2,200 characters. Limits change, so treat these as guides.
- **Web pages**: search engines shorten long titles and meta descriptions in results, so writers aim for roughly 50 to 60 characters for titles and 150 to 160 for descriptions.
- **Freelance writing and translation**: many clients pay per word, so both sides need an agreed count.

## Word count vs character count: which one should you use?

It depends on who sets the limit. This quick table helps.

| Situation | Usually measured in | Tip |
|---|---|---|
| Essays, SOPs, articles | Words | Ask whether headings and references are included |
| Social posts, SMS, ads | Characters (with spaces) | Leave a 5–10 character margin |
| Form fields on websites | Characters | Some forms count line breaks as two characters |
| Translation quotes | Words or characters without spaces | Confirm the unit before quoting a price |
| Speeches and voice-overs | Speaking time | Rehearse aloud; the estimate is a starting point |

## Why do different tools give different counts?

You may notice that Microsoft Word, Google Docs and online counters disagree by a few words. That is normal, because each one has its own rules for edge cases:

- **Hyphenated words** like "well-known" or "co-operative" may be one word or two.
- **Numbers and symbols** such as "₹5,000" or "10%" may or may not count as words.
- **Emoji and Indic text** can be counted as one character or several, depending on how the tool handles Unicode.
- **URLs and email addresses** are sometimes split at every dot.

The BISCS counter uses the browser's built-in text segmentation, which follows international Unicode rules. That is why Hindi, Bengali or Tamil words, and characters like "क्ष", are counted as you would expect rather than being split into pieces. If you are submitting to a portal that has its own counter, always check the final count there as well.

## How to use keyword density without overdoing it

The keyword table is useful for two different jobs.

For **writers and students**, it reveals habits. If "very", "basically" or your main subject appears far more than anything else, you probably need synonyms or tighter sentences.

For **bloggers and small businesses**, it shows whether your main topic actually comes through. A page about "GST registration" should mention the phrase naturally, but there is no magic percentage. Search engines reward pages that answer the question clearly, and repeating a phrase unnaturally can make the page read worse. ## Common mistakes when working to a word limit

- **Counting the wrong text.** Check whether the title, headings, footnotes and bibliography count towards the limit before you trim.
- **Pasting from a PDF.** Text copied from PDFs often carries broken lines and doubled spaces that inflate paragraph and line counts. Run it through the [Whitespace Cleaner](/tools/whitespace-cleaner/) first.
- **Trusting reading time blindly.** Technical notes, legal text and content in a second language take longer to read than 200 words per minute.
- **Cutting meaning to save words.** Remove repetition, filler and long introductions before you remove examples.
- **Ignoring the platform's own count.** Social apps and online forms may count emoji, links or line breaks differently from any external tool.

## Practical tips for trimming text quickly

If you are 50 to 100 words over, look for these first:

- Phrases like "in order to" (use "to"), "due to the fact that" (use "because") and "at this point in time" (use "now").
- Sentences that start with "It is important to note that". Usually the rest of the sentence stands alone.
- Repeated summaries at the start and end of each paragraph.
- Lists of three examples where two would do.
