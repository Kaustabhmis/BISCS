A readability checker estimates how easy a piece of writing is to read. It looks at things you can measure, mainly how long your sentences are and how long your words are, and turns them into scores such as Flesch Reading Ease or a school grade level.

The [Readability Checker](/tools/readability-checker/) from BISCS India gives you six standard scores at once, highlights the sentences and phrases that make text harder, and suggests specific fixes for the audience you choose. It runs in your browser, so drafts stay private.

## Why readability matters online

People skim on the web, often on a phone, between other tasks. Short sentences and familiar words are understood faster and abandoned less often. That is true for a product description, a government scheme explainer or an email to clients.

Clear writing also helps machines. Search engines and AI assistants extract and summarise passages; a crisp 18-word sentence is far easier to quote correctly than a 50-word sentence with three clauses. Readability is not a direct Google ranking factor as far as Google has said, but it shapes how people and answer engines use your content.

## What the scores mean

| Score | What it measures | Good target for the web |
|---|---|---|
| Flesch Reading Ease | 0–100; higher is easier, from sentence and syllable length | 60+ for general readers |
| Flesch-Kincaid grade | US school grade needed to follow the text | Around 7–9 |
| Gunning Fog | Grade estimate weighted by complex words | Below 12 |
| SMOG | Grade estimate from polysyllabic words (needs 3+ sentences) | Below 10–12 |
| Coleman-Liau | Grade from letters per word and sentences | Around 8–10 |
| ARI | Grade from characters per word and words per sentence | Around 8–10 |

The Flesch result also gets a plain label, from "Very easy" through "Plain English" to "Very difficult", with a note on who can read it comfortably.

All these formulas use English syllable and character counts, so treat them as guides rather than exact measurements, and use them for English text.

## Practical ways to improve a score

**Split long sentences.** Look for "and", "which", "because" and semicolons; they are natural breaking points. Two 15-word sentences beat one 30-word sentence.

**Choose everyday words.** "Use" instead of "utilise", "help" instead of "facilitate", "about" instead of "approximately". In Indian business writing, phrases like "kindly do the needful" or "please revert back" can usually be replaced with a direct request.

**Define jargon once.** "Input tax credit (ITC)" the first time, then ITC. Readers outside your field will thank you.

**Prefer active voice.** "We will send your invoice today" is shorter and clearer than "Your invoice will be sent today".

**Cut filler adverbs.** "Very", "really" and "actually" rarely add meaning. A stronger verb often does the job.

**Use structure.** Headings, short paragraphs and bullet lists do not change the formulas much, but they make text far easier to read.

## Common readability mistakes

- **Chasing a perfect 100.** Text written only in three-word sentences sounds childish. Mix short and medium sentences for a natural rhythm.
- **Ignoring the audience.** A tax guide for chartered accountants can be denser than one for first-time ITR filers. Pick the right audience setting.
- **Trusting every highlight.** Passive voice and adverb detection are approximate; some flags will be fine as they are.
- **Checking non-English text.** The scores are designed for English and will be misleading for other languages.
- **Editing out precision.** Never remove necessary legal or technical terms just to raise a score; explain them instead.
