## Worked examples

### Height of a building with trigonometry

Standing 40 m from a building, you measure the angle to the top as 35°. Height = 40 × tan(35°). In DEG mode, type `40*tan(35)` to get about **28.01 m**.

### Pythagoras

A right triangle has sides 3 and 4. The hypotenuse is `√(3^2+4^2)` = **5**. The angle opposite the side of 3 is `tan⁻¹(3/4)`, about **36.87°**.

### Compound growth

₹1,00,000 growing at 7% a year for 5 years becomes `100000*(1.07)^5`, about **₹1,40,255.17**. For full investment projections, a dedicated SIP or lumpsum calculator is more practical, but this is a handy check.

### Logs

`log(1000)` gives 3 and `ln(e)` gives 1. To find a log in any base, divide: log₂(64) is `log(64)/log(2)` = 6.

### Factorials and combinations

The number of ways to choose 3 students from 10 is 10! ÷ (3! × 7!). Type `10!/(3!*7!)` to get **120**.

## Order of operations, explained

The calculator follows standard maths rules, often taught as BODMAS:

1. **Brackets** first.
2. **Powers and roots**, worked from right to left, so `2^3^2` is 2^9 = 512.
3. **Multiplication and division**, left to right.
4. **Addition and subtraction**, left to right.

A minus sign in front of a power applies after the power, so `−3^2` is −9. If you mean (−3)², type `(−3)^2` to get 9. You can also write implied multiplication such as `2π` or `3(4+5)`.

## Common mistakes on scientific calculators

- **Wrong angle mode.** sin(30) is 0.5 in degrees but about −0.988 in radians. Check DEG/RAD first.
- **Missing brackets.** `1/2π` is read as (1/2) × π, about 1.571, while `1/(2π)` is about 0.159. Add brackets when in doubt.
- **Confusing log and ln.** log is base 10; ln is base e.
- **Taking logs of zero or negatives.** These are undefined, and the calculator tells you so.
- **tan(90°).** It is undefined, and the calculator says so instead of showing a huge number.
- **Factorials of decimals.** n! here needs a whole number from 0 upwards.

## Degrees or radians: which should you use?

Angles can be measured in two units. A full circle is 360 degrees, or 2π radians, so 180° equals π radians and 1 radian is about 57.3°.

Use **DEG** for school geometry, surveying, construction, navigation and most problems where an angle is given with a degree sign. Use **RAD** for calculus, physics formulas involving angular speed or oscillation, and anything where angles are written in terms of π, such as sin(π/6).

The setting changes how sin, cos and tan read their input, and how the inverse functions return their answer. If you switch mid-calculation, recalculate the steps that involved angles. A quick sanity check: in DEG mode, sin(90) should be exactly 1.
