A loan prepayment saves you interest because it lowers the principal immediately, and every future month’s interest is worked out on that smaller balance. If you keep the EMI the same and let the loan end sooner, the saving is usually much larger than if you keep the tenure and lower the EMI.

For example, on a ₹30 lakh home loan at 8.5% for 20 years, the EMI is ₹26,035 and the total interest over the full term is ₹32,48,327. A single prepayment of ₹2,00,000 in month 12 saves ₹6,87,516 of interest if you choose to reduce the tenure, and the loan closes 2 years 10 months early. The free [Loan Prepayment Calculator](/tools/loan-prepayment-calculator/) shows these figures side by side so you can test your own plan.

## How does prepaying a loan reduce interest?

Most Indian home, car and personal loans are reducing-balance loans. Each month the lender charges interest on what you still owe, and the rest of your EMI goes towards principal. In the early years the balance is high, so a large share of each EMI is interest.

When you prepay, the extra amount goes straight to principal. The interest charged next month is lower, so more of the same EMI clears principal, which lowers the following month’s interest further. This chain effect is why a modest lump sum can remove several years of payments.

After a prepayment, lenders typically give you two choices:

- **Reduce tenure (keep EMI):** you pay the same instalment and the loan ends earlier.
- **Reduce EMI (keep tenure):** the lender recalculates a smaller instalment over the remaining months.

## Worked example: ₹30 lakh home loan at 8.5%

Using the calculator’s default loan of ₹30 lakh for 20 years at 8.5%, here is what different prepayment plans do. All figures come from the tool.

| Plan | Interest saved | Effect on loan |
|---|---|---|
| ₹2,00,000 once in month 12, reduce tenure | ₹6,87,516 | Ends 2 yrs 10 mo early (17 yrs 2 mo) |
| ₹2,00,000 once in month 12, reduce EMI | ₹2,03,763 | EMI falls from ₹26,035 to ₹24,264 |
| ₹2,00,000 once in month 120, reduce tenure | ₹2,40,758 | Ends 1 yr 4 mo early |
| ₹5,000 extra every month, reduce tenure | ₹11,73,056 | Loan lasts 13 yrs 8 mo |
| ₹1,00,000 extra every year, reduce tenure | ₹14,84,494 | Loan lasts 11 yrs 9 mo |

Three lessons stand out. First, the same ₹2 lakh saves more than three times as much when you shorten the tenure rather than the EMI. Second, timing matters: that ₹2 lakh is worth roughly ₹6.9 lakh in year one but only about ₹2.4 lakh in year ten. Third, small regular prepayments add up; ₹5,000 a month cuts more than six years off the loan.

The **Return on each ₹1 prepaid** figure is a handy way to compare plans. A higher number means each rupee you put in is clearing more future interest.

## Reduce tenure or reduce EMI: which should you pick?

Pick **reduce tenure** if your current EMI is comfortable and your goal is to become debt-free and pay the least interest. This is the option most people should consider first.

Pick **reduce EMI** if your monthly budget is tight, your income is uncertain, or you want more room for other goals such as a child’s education. A lower EMI also leaves a bigger cushion if a floating rate rises later.

A middle path is to reduce the EMI now and then voluntarily pay a little extra each month. You keep flexibility while still saving a fair amount of interest.

## Prepayment charges and RBI rules

RBI does not allow banks and other regulated lenders to charge foreclosure or part-prepayment penalties on floating-rate term loans given to individual borrowers for purposes other than business. Most home loans in India today are floating-rate, so this covers a large number of borrowers.

Fixed-rate loans, some car and personal loans, and loans taken for business can still carry a charge, often a percentage of the amount prepaid. Check the prepayment clause in your sanction letter or loan agreement. If you enter a charge, the calculator shows **Prepayment charges** and **Net saving**, and warns you if the fee is larger than the interest saved.

## Tips before you prepay

- **Keep an emergency fund.** Money paid into a loan is hard to get back quickly. Hold several months of expenses aside before prepaying.
- **Prepay early in the loan.** The first five to seven years are when each rupee saves the most interest.
- **Use bonuses and increments.** An **Extra every year** amount timed with your annual bonus is easy to sustain.
- **Tell the lender what you want.** Confirm in writing whether the prepayment should reduce tenure or EMI, and check the revised schedule.
- **Clear costlier loans first.** If you also have a personal loan or credit card balance, the higher-rate debt usually deserves the money before a home loan.

## Mistakes to avoid

1. **Ignoring the tax angle.** Interest on a self-occupied home loan may be deductible under section 24(b), but this is mainly relevant if you file under the old tax regime. Prepaying reduces that interest, so the real saving can be a little smaller for some borrowers. Compare both regimes in the [Income Tax Calculator](/tools/income-tax-calculator/) and speak to a tax professional for your situation.
2. **Assuming the rate never changes.** The calculator assumes a fixed rate. On a floating loan, rerun it after each rate reset using your current balance.
3. **Entering the wrong month.** The one-time payment month counts from the loan start date, so add your EMIs already paid.
4. **Draining all savings.** A prepayment that leaves you borrowing on a credit card later costs more than it saves.
5. **Forgetting the original EMI.** To see your base instalment and amortisation before planning a prepayment, start with the [EMI Calculator](/tools/emi-calculator/).

## What the calculator assumes

The tool works on a monthly reducing balance, applies each prepayment right after that month’s EMI, and keeps the interest rate fixed. Lenders may use slightly different rounding, day counts or processing dates, so your bank’s revised schedule can differ by a small amount. Treat the results as a planning estimate, not financial advice, and confirm the final numbers with your lender.
