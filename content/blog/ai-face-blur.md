Blurring faces hides people’s identities in a photo while keeping the rest of the scene. It is a simple, respectful step before sharing a group picture on social media, posting a street scene, publishing an event report or using a photo that includes children who are not your own.

The [AI Face Blur](/tools/ai-face-blur/) tool from BISCS India detects faces automatically, lets you choose which ones to hide, and offers blur, pixelation, a solid cover or an emoji sticker. Detection runs in your browser, so the photo never leaves your device.

## When should you blur faces before sharing?

- **School and coaching events:** parents and teachers often post annual-day or sports-day photos. Other families may not want their children online.
- **Housing society and community events:** a Durga Puja pandal, a Holi gathering or a resident meeting captures many neighbours who never agreed to be posted.
- **Street and travel photography:** passers-by in a market scene are incidental, and blurring them respects their privacy.
- **Reports and complaints:** a photo of a civic problem, such as a broken road or garbage dump, may include bystanders who have nothing to do with it.
- **Screenshots of video calls** shared outside the meeting.
- **Workplace documentation:** site photos, training sessions and office events used in presentations or on websites.

Privacy expectations and laws vary, and India’s data protection framework continues to evolve, so when in doubt, ask for permission or hide the face.

## How to blur faces step by step

1. Open [AI Face Blur](/tools/ai-face-blur/) and choose a photo, drop it in or paste it with Ctrl + V.
2. On first use, the AI engine and face model download once (about 10 MB). After that, detection starts straight away.
3. Under **Detection**, keep **Group / distant** for group shots and crowds; it scans the photo in tiles to find smaller faces. Choose **Close-up / selfie** for photos with one or two large faces.
4. Review the boxes. Click a box, or untick it in the **Faces** list, to leave that face visible. Use **All** or **None** to switch every face at once.
5. **Drag on the photo** to add a box over any face that was missed. Remove a wrong box with its delete button.
6. Choose an **Effect**: Blur, Pixelate, Solid or Emoji. Set the **Strength**, **Box padding** and **Shape** (oval, rounded or square).
7. Untick **Show face boxes** to see the clean result, choose JPG or PNG, and download.

## Blur, pixelate, solid or emoji?

| Effect | Look | Privacy strength | Good for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blur | Soft, natural | Good at high strength | Social posts, news-style images |
| Pixelate | Mosaic blocks | Good with small blocks and padding | Documentation, a clearly “redacted” look |
| Solid | Plain colour shape | Strongest, nothing visible | Sensitive situations, minors, legal or HR use |
| Emoji | Fun sticker | Strong if it covers the whole face | Family and friends’ posts |

If someone’s identity truly matters, for example a child in a vulnerable situation, use **Solid** with generous padding. Blur and pixelation at low strength can sometimes leave a face recognisable to people who know the person.

## Honest limits of automatic face detection

The detector is a compact model designed to run quickly on phones and laptops. It works well for clear faces, but it can miss:

- **Small, distant faces** at the back of a crowd or a large hall, even with the group mode’s tiling.
- **Faces in profile** or turned away, and faces partly hidden by hands, phones, hair or other people.
- **Faces with masks, helmets or heavy shadow.**
- **Faces in posters, TV screens or reflections** in mirrors and glass.

It can also occasionally mark something that is not a face. So treat the automatic result as a first pass. Zoom in, scan the whole photo, and add manual boxes wherever needed. Each detected face shows a confidence percentage in the list; low scores deserve a closer look.

## A note for schools, coaching centres and parents

Photos of children deserve extra care. Many schools and institutions now ask for parental consent before publishing pictures of students, and some parents prefer that their children never appear online at all. If you manage a school’s social media page, a coaching institute’s website or a class WhatsApp group that sometimes forwards photos publicly, a simple routine helps:

- Keep a list of students whose photos should not be shared, and blur them consistently.
- Prefer photos taken from behind or at a distance for public posts about events.
- Use the **Solid** or **Emoji** effect for children rather than light blur, so nothing recognisable remains.
- Check name badges, ID cards on lanyards and uniforms with the school name.

Blurring is quick, and it lets you share the joy of an event without putting anyone’s privacy at risk.

## Tips for reliable anonymisation

- **Use padding.** Hair, ears and the outline of the head help people recognise someone. A padding of 15% or more covers them.
- **Hide other identifiers.** Name tags, school logos, house numbers and vehicle number plates can be covered with a manual box and the Solid effect.
- **Keep the original private.** Share the blurred copy, and store the original securely or delete it.
- **Check at full size.** Something that looks hidden on a phone screen can be visible when the image is opened large.

## Common mistakes

- **Trusting the detector blindly** and posting without checking the background.
- **Low blur strength on big faces.** Increase it until features are truly unreadable.
- **Forgetting reflections and screens** in the photo.
- **Posting the original first and the blurred version later.** Once shared, the original may already be saved by others.
