A recurring deposit (RD) lets you save a fixed amount every month and earn a fixed interest rate, like a fixed deposit paid in instalments. Its maturity value is the sum of every instalment plus the interest each one earns while it sits in the account, usually compounded quarterly.

If you deposit ₹5,000 a month for 5 years at 7%, you put in ₹3,00,000 and receive about ₹3,59,664 at maturity. The free [RD Calculator](/tools/rd-calculator/) on BISCS India works this out for any amount, rate and tenure, and shows how the balance grows each year.

## How is the RD maturity amount calculated?

Think of an RD as many small FDs opened one month apart. For a tenure of n months and annual rate r, the instalment paid in month k stays deposited for (n − k + 1) months, which is (n − k + 1) ÷ 3 quarters. Its value at maturity is:

**instalment × (1 + r ÷ 400)^((n − k + 1) ÷ 3)**

The calculator adds this up for every month from the first to the last. That is how Indian banks typically describe RD interest: quarterly compounding applied to each instalment for the time it is held.

The practical result is that early instalments earn far more than late ones. In a 60-month RD, the first ₹5,000 earns interest for five full years, while the last ₹5,000 earns for just one month.

| ₹5,000 | 7% | 5 years | ₹3,00,000 | ₹3,59,664 | ₹59,664 |

The interest in the first row may look small next to an FD at the same rate. That is because the average rupee in an RD is deposited for only about half the tenure. A ₹60,000 FD for a year at 7% would earn more, but you would need the whole ₹60,000 on day one.

## When does an RD make sense?

- **Saving towards a known expense** such as school fees due next April, a Diwali purchase or an insurance premium.
- **Building a habit.** A standing instruction from your salary account turns saving into something automatic.
- **Low risk tolerance.** The return is fixed at the start, so market moves do not affect it.
- **Short horizons.** For goals one to three years away, the certainty of an RD often matters more than squeezing out extra return.

For longer goals, compare an RD with a [SIP Calculator](/tools/sip-calculator/) estimate, keeping in mind that SIP returns are not guaranteed. For tax-free long-term saving, a PPF account is another option; see the [PPF Calculator](/tools/ppf-calculator/).

## Checklist before opening an RD

- Confirm whether the rate quoted is for your exact tenure; rates can differ by a few months either side.
- Ask what happens if you miss an instalment, and how late payments are charged.
- Check premature closure rules and the penalty rate.
- Set the instalment date a few days after your salary credit to avoid a failed debit.
- Remember the interest is taxable, so plan for tax in your return.
- If you also hold FDs at the same bank, add up the expected interest; TDS thresholds apply to your total deposit interest there.

## Mistakes to avoid

1. **Treating the maturity value as "returns".** Most of it is your own money. In the 5-year example, the gain is ₹59,664 on ₹3 lakh deposited.
2. **Comparing an RD rate directly with an FD's maturity.** Use the same total money and timeline when comparing. The [FD Calculator](/tools/fd-calculator/) helps with the lump-sum side.
3. **Choosing too long a tenure.** If you might need the money early, premature closure usually reduces the rate.
4. **Ignoring inflation and tax.** At typical slab rates, the post-tax return on an RD can be close to inflation.

## Limits of this estimate

The tool follows the standard quarterly-compounding method most banks describe, but individual banks and the Post Office scheme can differ in detail: the exact compounding schedule, how a final part-quarter is handled and rounding at each step. Small differences of a few rupees from your bank's statement are normal.

Use the result for planning, not as a commitment from any bank. This article is general information rather than financial advice. Confirm the current RD rate and terms with your bank or India Post, and check TDS rules on the Income Tax Department website.
