To convert a colour PDF to black and white, open [Grayscale PDF](/tools/grayscale-pdf/), choose a resolution (150 DPI suits most uses) and an image quality, then convert and download. The page thumbnails already preview in grey, so you can see the result before you start.

The conversion runs entirely in your browser, so the document is never uploaded. One trade-off to know: each page becomes a grayscale image, so the text is no longer selectable or searchable.

## Why convert a PDF to grayscale?

There are practical reasons beyond saving colour ink:

- **Cheaper printing.** Print shops and office printers often charge more for colour pages. A grayscale file makes sure no page is accidentally printed in colour.
- **Portal or office requirements.** Some forms and submission instructions ask for black-and-white copies. Requirements vary, so check the specific instructions.
- **Consistent look.** A bundle of documents from different sources (blue bank statements, red-stamped certificates, green tax forms) looks uniform in grayscale.
- **Better readability of some scans.** Yellowed paper and colour casts from phone photos often look cleaner in grey.
- **Smaller files for colourful scans.** Colour photos of documents carry a lot of data; grayscale images usually need less.

## How to convert a PDF to grayscale step by step

1. Open [Grayscale PDF](/tools/grayscale-pdf/) and choose your PDF.
2. Look at the page grid; thumbnails are shown in grayscale as a preview.
3. Choose the **Resolution (DPI)**: 100, 150, 200 or 300.
4. Set the **Image quality** slider (the default is 80%).
5. Read the notice: pages will become images and text will not be selectable.
6. Convert and download. The result screen shows the page count and the original size so you can compare.

## Choosing resolution and quality

| Document type | Resolution | Quality | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Typed letters, forms, statements | 150 DPI | 75–80% | Clear text, moderate size |
| Small print, legal fine print, tables | 200–300 DPI | 80–90% | Keeps small characters sharp |
| Photo-heavy brochures | 150 DPI | 70–80% | Photos look fine, file stays reasonable |
| Quick draft for internal reading | 100 DPI | 70% | Smallest file, check legibility |

Higher DPI and quality give sharper pages and larger files. If the result is too big, try 150 DPI before reducing quality heavily.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Converting the only copy.** Grayscale is a one-way trip for practical purposes, and text becomes an image. Always keep the colour original.
- **Converting before filling forms.** Fillable form fields are flattened into the image. Fill the form first with [Fill PDF Form](/tools/fill-pdf-form/), then convert.
- **Using grayscale where colour carries meaning.** Charts with colour-coded lines, maps and medical images can become hard to read. Check the preview carefully.
- **Expecting a smaller file every time.** Text-only digital PDFs can get bigger, because vector text is replaced by pictures of text. Compare sizes on the result screen.
- **Choosing a low DPI for small print.** At 100 DPI, footnotes and fine print can blur. Zoom in on the result before submitting.

## Grayscale vs compress: which one do you need?

Both tools redraw pages as images, so they overlap. The difference is the goal:

- Use **Grayscale PDF** when colour must go, for printing or a black-and-white requirement.
- Use [Compress PDF](/tools/compress-pdf/) when you only need a smaller file and colour is fine. Its Basic level keeps text selectable, which grayscale cannot do.

If you need both (a black-and-white file under a size limit), convert to grayscale at 150 DPI first; the result is often small enough. If not, try a lower DPI or quality rather than running a second image-based compression on top.

## Example: printing a 200-page study pack

A coaching institute in Patna wants to print a 200-page colour PDF of study material for 60 students. Printing in colour would be costly, and the colour adds little to the text-heavy content. The administrator converts the PDF to grayscale at 150 DPI and 80% quality, checks a few pages with diagrams to make sure labels are readable, and sends the grayscale file to the print shop. Because the file contains no colour at all, there is no risk of any page being billed as colour.

## Tips for the best result

- Faint pencil marks, light stamps and pale signatures may become lighter in grey. If they matter, raise the DPI and quality and check those pages.
- Coloured highlights turn into grey bands. They usually remain visible, but very light yellow can almost disappear.
- For documents photographed on a phone, a proper scan with [Scan to PDF](/tools/scan-to-pdf/) and its Gray or B&W filter often gives a cleaner page than converting a colour photo afterwards.

## Checklist before you print or submit

- Zoom in on the smallest text in the grayscale file and confirm it is crisp.
- Check pages with signatures, seals and stamps; they should still be clearly visible.
- Look at charts and diagrams to make sure lines that relied on colour are still distinguishable.
- Compare the new file size with the original if a portal limit applies.
- Keep the colour original saved separately for future edits.

## What about documents that must stay searchable?

If someone needs to search or copy text from the final document, grayscale conversion is not ideal, because the output is image-based. In that case, print the colour PDF using your printer's "grayscale" or "black and white" setting instead, which saves ink without changing the file. Use Grayscale PDF when the file itself must be black and white, for example when you hand it over to a print shop or submit it somewhere.

## Privacy and performance

Each page is rendered and converted to grey using your browser's own graphics engine, then reassembled into a new PDF on your device. Nothing is uploaded. Long documents at 300 DPI are processed page by page with a progress bar, and they take noticeably longer on a phone than on a computer, so for big files a laptop is the comfortable choice.
