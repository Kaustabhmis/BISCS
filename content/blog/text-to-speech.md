Text to speech (TTS) turns written text into spoken audio. Paste a paragraph, choose a voice and press play, and your device reads it aloud. It is useful for proofreading, learning pronunciation, listening to long articles while you do something else, and making text accessible to people who find reading difficult.

The BISCS India tool uses the voices already built into your phone or computer, so it works without an account and without sending your text to our servers. That also means the voices you get depend on your device.

## How to use text to speech online

1. Open [Text to Speech](/tools/text-to-speech/).
2. Type or paste your text. The word count and estimated listening time appear above the box.
3. Pick a **Voice**. Indian English (en-IN) and Hindi (hi-IN) voices are grouped at the top under "Indian voices".
4. Adjust **Speed** (0.5× to 2×), **Pitch** and **Volume** with the sliders.
5. Press **Play**. The text appears in a reading panel with each word highlighted as it is spoken.
6. Use **Pause**, **Resume** and **Stop** as needed.

Your chosen voice is remembered on that device for next time.

## Which voices will you get?

The voice list comes from your operating system and browser, so it differs from one device to another.

| Device | Typical voices | How to add more |
|---|---|---|
| Android phone (Chrome) | Google voices, often including English (India) and Hindi | System settings, text-to-speech output, install voice data |
| Windows PC (Edge or Chrome) | Microsoft voices; Edge may also list online voices | Windows language and speech settings |
| iPhone or iPad (Safari) | Apple voices including Indian English options | Settings, Accessibility, Spoken Content, Voices |
| Mac (Safari or Chrome) | Apple system voices | System Settings, Accessibility, Spoken Content |

After installing a new voice, reload the page so the browser picks it up. If no voices are reported at all, the tool falls back to your system's default voice.

### What does "online" next to a voice mean?

Some browsers provide extra, often more natural-sounding voices that run on the browser maker's servers. The tool labels these "online". They can sound better, but your text is sent to that service to be spoken. For private documents such as medical reports or contracts, choose a voice without the online label so speech is generated on your device.

## Reading Hindi and mixed Hinglish text

Hindi voices read Devanagari text well. Mixed text is trickier:

- A Hindi voice may read English words with a Hindi accent, or spell out unfamiliar ones.
- An Indian English voice will usually skip or mispronounce Devanagari text.
- Hinglish written in Roman script ("aap kaise ho") is read as English and may sound odd.

For best results, match the voice to the main language of the text. If a document mixes both, split it and read each part with the right voice.

## Practical uses

**Proofreading.** Hearing your writing read back is one of the best ways to catch missing words, repeated phrases and clumsy sentences. Your brain skips mistakes when reading silently, but not when listening.

**Learning and revision.** Students can listen to notes while travelling, and language learners can hear how English or Hindi words are pronounced at a slower speed.

**Accessibility.** People with low vision, dyslexia or reading fatigue can listen instead of reading. Older family members can have a WhatsApp forward or a bill read out to them.

**Checking speech timing.** Paste a speech or presentation script, set the speed to match your pace, and see the estimated speaking time. The [Word Counter](/tools/word-counter/) gives a speaking time estimate too.

**Listening to articles.** Copy an article and listen while cooking or commuting. For text inside a PDF, extract it first with [PDF to Text](/tools/pdf-to-text/), or use [Image to Text](/tools/image-to-text/) for a photo or scan.

## Common problems and fixes

- **No sound.** Check that the device is not muted, that the Volume slider is up, and that Bluetooth headphones are connected to the right device.
- **Speech stops part-way.** Some browsers stop when the screen locks or the tab is in the background, especially on phones. Keep the tab open and the screen on.
- **Strange pronunciation of numbers and abbreviations.** Write "Rs" or "rupees" instead of relying on the ₹ symbol, and expand short forms like "approx." if the voice stumbles.
- **Robotic sound.** Try a different voice. Quality varies a lot, and newer voices often sound more natural.
- **Highlighting stays on the whole sentence.** That voice does not report word positions. Choose another voice if you want word-by-word highlighting.

## Tips for better listening

- Clean pasted text first. Broken line breaks from PDFs can cause unnatural pauses, so run it through the [Whitespace Cleaner](/tools/whitespace-cleaner/).
- Use a speed of 1.2× to 1.5× for familiar material and 0.8× for learning or difficult text.
- Add full stops and commas where you want pauses; voices follow punctuation.
- Keep a lower pitch for long sessions, which many people find easier to listen to.

## A quick routine for proofreading by ear

Writers, students and office staff can turn text to speech into a simple editing habit:

1. Finish your draft and paste it into the tool.
2. Choose a clear voice and a speed slightly below normal, around 0.9×.
3. Follow the highlighted words with your eyes while listening.
4. Pause whenever something sounds wrong: a missing "the", a doubled word, a sentence that runs out of breath.
5. Fix the issue in your original document, then carry on.

It works especially well for emails to clients, cover letters and assignment answers, where a single missing word can change the meaning. Ten minutes of listening often catches mistakes that three silent readings missed.

## What the tool cannot do

Be clear about the limits so you pick the right tool:

- It **cannot save audio files**. Browsers play speech directly and do not give websites the audio, so there is no MP3 download.
- Voice quality and languages depend entirely on your device.
- It reads plain text, not formatted documents, so headings and tables are read as ordinary sentences.

For listening, proofreading and accessibility, though, [Text to Speech](/tools/text-to-speech/) is quick, free and private, with nothing to install.
