The quickest way is to type the new size, keep the aspect ratio locked so nothing gets stretched, and download.

## When do you actually need to resize a photo?

Most phone cameras now save pictures at 12 megapixels or more, around 4000 × 3000 pixels. That is far more than most uses need, and it causes real problems:

- **Online application forms.** Government recruitment portals, university admissions and bank exam sites often ask for a photo and signature within fixed pixel dimensions. Upload a full-size phone photo and the portal rejects it.
- **Websites and blogs.** A 4000-pixel hero image slows the page down for no visible benefit on a 1920-pixel-wide screen.
- **Social media.** Platforms crop or recompress images that do not match their preferred sizes, sometimes badly.
- **Email and chat.** Smaller images send faster on mobile data and take less space in your recipient’s inbox.

If your problem is the file size in KB rather than the dimensions, you need compression as well. The [Compress Image](/tools/image-compressor/) tool can hit a target size such as 50 KB, and it is often best used after resizing.

## Understanding aspect ratio (and why photos get squashed)

The aspect ratio is the relationship between width and height. A typical phone photo is 4:3, so a 4000 × 3000 image resized to 1200 wide becomes 1200 × 900. If you then force it to 1200 × 1200, something has to give.

**Stretch** squeezes the picture into the new box, which makes people look wider or thinner. It is rarely what you want. **Crop to fill** scales the photo until the box is covered and trims the overflow from the edges. **Fit inside** scales the photo until it fits completely and fills the leftover space, so nothing is cut off.

For portraits and passport-style photos, crop to fill usually looks best. For logos, screenshots and documents, fit inside is safer because nothing important gets trimmed. If you want to choose exactly what gets trimmed, crop by hand first with [Crop Image](/tools/image-cropper/), then resize.

## Resizing photos for Indian exam and job application forms

Recruitment and admission portals in India commonly ask for a recent colour photograph and a scanned signature, each with its own pixel dimensions, file format and size range in KB. The exact numbers are not universal. They differ between SSC, UPSC, banking, railway and state commission notifications, and they can change from one recruitment cycle to the next.

So before resizing, open the official notification or the portal’s instructions and note three things: the pixel dimensions (if given), the file format (usually JPG) and the minimum and maximum size in KB. Then:

- Resize to the stated dimensions using pixel mode with crop to fill.
- If the result is above the KB limit, run it through the compressor with a target size.
- If it is below the minimum KB, raise the quality during compression rather than enlarging the image.

For signatures, sign in dark ink on plain white paper, photograph it in good light, crop tightly around the signature and then resize. The [Signature Maker](/tools/signature-maker/) can also create a clean digital signature if the form accepts one.

## Practical tips for better results

- **Resize once, from the original.** Every round of resizing and re-saving a JPG loses a little quality. Keep your original and always start from it.
- **Downsizing is safe, upsizing is not.** Going from 4000 to 1200 pixels looks crisp. Going from 400 to 1200 looks blurry because the extra pixels are guessed. For enlargements, the [AI Image Upscaler](/tools/ai-image-upscaler/) reconstructs detail more convincingly.
- **Use even numbers for video and design work.** Some video encoders and layout systems prefer even widths and heights.
- **Mind the format.** Resizing does not change the file type. If you need a JPG from a PNG screenshot, convert it as well.
- **Large batches on a phone take longer.** Everything runs on your device, so a budget phone handling fifty 12-megapixel photos will need a little patience. A laptop is quicker for big jobs.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Typing both width and height with the lock off and ending up with distorted faces.
- Resizing a photo to the right dimensions but forgetting the KB limit, then getting rejected anyway.
- Enlarging a small WhatsApp-forwarded photo for printing and expecting it to look sharp.
- Cropping out part of the head or chin in an application photo because crop to fill trimmed more than expected. Check the preview before downloading.
- Repeatedly editing and saving the same JPG instead of going back to the original.
